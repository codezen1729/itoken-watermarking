Have the host meet guests as they come in, ask their name and whether they feel like playing yet, and put both answers on the welcome sheet. That greeting gives a first-time guest someone to come back to, and the host knows who is looking for a seat. Invite people to browse the games and leave a marker by anything that catches their eye. Use the interests of the people actually in the room to make the evening’s suggestions. Other available titles can still be requested.

When there is room to start a game, willing teachers call out what they can teach. Let guests gather around, meet the teacher and ask what the game involves. The host checks that the people choosing to play fall within the player range on the box. Anyone can pass and wait for another invitation. A welcome volunteer can introduce a latecomer to a group nearly finished with its game and make a note if they agree to play the next one together. That gives the guest a group to look forward to while the current players finish. The host brings waiting guests over to a short activity already in progress and comes back to tell the group when another game can start. People can enjoy each other’s company while the host keeps track of openings.

At a new table the teacher should go over the essentials with everyone together, following the printed aid, and then invite questions about what they have just heard. Let the group call out moves to try while the teacher plays out a few turns. Seeing the suggestions in action lets everyone check their understanding together. The teacher resets the pieces before scoring starts. During the game, someone new to it can ask the teacher for advice whenever they want it. The teacher can talk through the costs and benefits apparent from the information those players can see, leaving the player to choose the move.

Write the agreed stopping time where everyone at the table can see it and choose a player to give notice as that deadline approaches. Allow time to record an unfinished position if desired and pack the box. Separately, set a timer in view of the players for checkpoints they agree on. At each ring, take a moment to see how much time remains and discuss progress. If a rule causes a dispute, the table pauses while two players spend a short time looking it up. Accept the pair’s reading, or their provisional decision for the evening if the wording remains unclear. The others need only hear the outcome, and an open question can be checked later.

A player wanting a drink or snack should agree on a brief absence with the table, use the refreshment area and come back before their next action. This accommodates individual needs and keeps the pieces dry. For a personal break, speak up before leaving and agree on a place to pause right away. Everyone should know the action to resume with, keep private information covered and wait for the player where their participation is needed. As the host goes around the room, listen for neighboring tables having trouble hearing their own play. A quiet word from the host asking a loud group to lower its voices saves the neighbors having to raise it themselves.

After each game one player reads off the contents list in order. The others collect and count the pieces into the labeled bags, with the reader checking off each group before the box goes back. The next day the host sends an optional short prompt asking about the choice of games, finding a table and any difficulty taking part. Group the replies when planning the next evening so guests have had a chance to reflect and experiences common to several different tables are easy to see.
