Arrange a group booking in a single conversation between the organizer and museum staff. Work out what the group would enjoy, which rooms it can visit and when a volunteer can accompany it, allowing both parties to revise the arrangements as they talk. Divide weekend service into short periods with a dedicated reception volunteer and a dedicated interpreter for the whole of each period. Visitors can find someone for directions on arrival, and anyone discussing a work can stay with the volunteer who began the conversation.

Ask a new arrival what they are interested in, then show them a suitable first room on the map. With a repeat visitor, find out which room stood out on their previous visit. Point them toward a related object in a room that will be new to them. If time is tight, let the visitor choose appealing highlights on the guide sheet first. Use the estimated viewing times to help narrow the list around their favorites.

Try a suitable route from the map with a small party. After it has gone through, ask the room volunteers about crowding and decide whether the next party should follow it. Do that again for every group so the suggested routes reflect conditions in the galleries. A guide leading a tour should arrive with a short themed itinerary. Before the group departs, describe it and adapt it to what the visitors say they would most like to linger over.

Invite visitors to compare pieces in different locations by repeating a question on each piece’s label. Save the explanation of the connection for a label at the end, giving people time to form their own ideas. For an object associated with a local story, place the account before the encounter with the object and a question for reflection after it. People can then look for details they have just read about. Design the folded activity sheet for independent visitors so they look closely at the piece first. Unfolding the sheet reveals the writing prompt once they have had a chance to notice details for themselves.

When a group pauses to talk, the room volunteer should lead with a question. After people have discussed it, ask them to put any questions the exchange suggested on cards. At a display that brings back memories, allow time to write a recollection on a card and leave it at the exhibit. The writer can choose whether to share it in conversation with a volunteer. For other feedback, include the visitor route on the comment card and let people mark where something held their attention so the team can place their account.

Near closing time, one volunteer should update the time left on the reception display while others give reminders in the rooms. To improve a description, try revised text on display and keep track of questions that recur once it is in place. These point to explanations visitors still need. Bring one modest proposed change to the weekend review. Consider what room attendance, visitor feedback and the volunteers’ firsthand observations each suggest would happen in a trial, and look for agreement across the three before deciding to go ahead.
