At weekend reception, ask what drew the visitor to the museum and use their answer to show them a promising first room on the map. With returning visitors, find out which room they especially liked. Consult the connections list for an object elsewhere that follows up that interest. Someone on a bus schedule can choose what appeals to them from the guide sheet, then go over the estimated viewing times with reception and trim the list to fit.

At the welcome table, each of the two volunteers should meet a small group and continue answering that group's first questions about the collection. People will recognize the person helping them as they begin exploring. Before a short guided tour leaves, offer two themes that can be illustrated in the rooms and invite the group to settle on one. When a group arranges a visit ahead of time, give its organizer room descriptions and the hours volunteers are available to accompany them. With that information the organizer can select the focus.

Leave the route map out at reception all day. When a room gets crowded, the volunteer there can approach people already in the galleries and point out a quieter way to go from their current location. For a sequence of related objects, repeat one question on each object's label. Save the museum's account of how the examples fit together for the final label so people have a chance to compare them for themselves.

Arrange the local history display so visitors encounter the written story before the object. Once they've read about the events, invite them to look at the object and respond to the reflection question. On the activity handout, put the instruction to look closely on the front and the writing prompt on the reverse for visitors to discover afterward. When something sparks a personal memory, a volunteer can sit down with the visitor to write the memory card during their conversation. Both have the opportunity to check a detail as they go.

Set out question cards ahead of the planned discussion break so people can use them while exploring. That lets the volunteer sort similar questions and take them up together at the break. Keep a record of recurring questions and use them to draft clearer answers the next time volunteers edit the room descriptions. On general feedback cards, ask for the room first, followed by the object or experience the visitor wants to describe. Comments on different aspects of a room can then be collected together.

As closing approaches, have one volunteer update the reception display while the room volunteers give the reminder where they are, reaching arriving visitors and people inside at the same time. In reviewing the weekend, select a comment that came from several visitors. Look at the counts for that room and the volunteers' observations before settling on a modest improvement that makes sense in light of all three accounts.
