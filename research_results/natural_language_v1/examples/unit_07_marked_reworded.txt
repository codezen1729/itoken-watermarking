Before the fair, recruit volunteers on each block to ask neighbors what they might bring. Have them report back by category with approximate quantities for planning table space. Talking at the door includes people who might never fill in a form. Check everything against the published criteria when it actually arrives. Let contributors select an arrival slot on a shared intake sheet, leaving some capacity for unscheduled arrivals in every period. Volunteers can see when to expect a rush. The coordinator uses volunteers' preferred duties and available hours to arrange shifts and circulates the roster in advance so people can prepare and uncovered shifts stand out.

At intake, a contributor stays until a volunteer has compared each item with the condition list. This gives them a chance to clear up a question about damage or an absent part before the volunteer accepts it. For every accepted item the volunteer interviews the contributor and writes up the standard label, recording the item's identifying reference, what it is, any limitations and the accessories that go with it. A volunteer completing the label from the answers helps keep descriptions consistent. Tie or bag the parts of complete sets together before sending them out to the floor, with the description visible. Visitors can still tell what's included while the pieces stay together through the afternoon.

Organize the tables by household room, with signs that direct someone after a saucepan to the kitchen goods or someone outfitting a bedroom to the bedroom goods. Leave the aisles open by storing excess goods on reserve shelves. A runner checks the tables at regular intervals and fills newly open spaces with suitable reserve stock, leaving the table volunteers free to attend to visitors.

As visitors come in, the welcome volunteer brings a few together at a time to explain how to look around, claim something, leave it on hold and collect it. Everyone is welcome to give or take without bringing something in exchange. Let the group begin browsing and use the entrance diagram to answer individual questions. Someone who can't find an item can post a request by the appropriate category sign. If a suitable item turns up, the table volunteer takes the request to a central collection point where visitors can look for a match. A request is no guarantee that the item will appear.

For competing requests, enter each person's request and the time on the sheet. Once the short decision period ends, award the item to the earliest recorded requester. The times are available for people to see how the volunteer decided. Anyone with a claim who wishes to continue looking around can bring their goods to the volunteer managing holds. That volunteer writes down the recipient's name and confirms a pickup time no more than twenty minutes away before putting the goods on a shelf. Both know who has the item and when it must be collected.

To take an item home, present the claim record to the volunteer at the outgoing register. The volunteer confirms the claim, enters the item's identifying reference and gives the recipient a checked slip to show on the way out. Complete those steps before handing over the goods. At that same point anyone can request carrying help without explaining why. When the item is ready, the volunteer assigns the next free helper and cart to take it to the nearby pickup area.

At closing, use each contributor's previously recorded instructions to sort the unclaimed items before letting contributors in to collect them. Arrange the returns for easy retrieval and put the donations together for their scheduled pickup after collection ends. Invite visitors, contributors and volunteers to give feedback in separate, brief conversations if they wish. Ask how it went finding and claiming goods and keeping the fair running. Afterward the organizers compare their notes to see how a procedure worked for people in different roles and which changes might benefit several groups.
