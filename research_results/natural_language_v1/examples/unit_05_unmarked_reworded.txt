Greet each arrival personally. The host asks what name to put on the welcome sheet and whether the guest is ready for a game, recording that answer too. A newcomer now has a familiar face to approach, and the host can tell who needs a place. Encourage guests to look over the games and mark those they fancy. Base suggestions for the evening on the markers left by the people attending, while keeping requests open for any title available.

As space opens, people willing to teach announce their games. Give guests time to gather, talk to a teacher and find out what playing entails. Guests are free to wait for a later invitation. For those who opt in, the host checks the number against the range printed on the box. If someone comes in late, a welcome volunteer can introduce them at a table close to finishing. If they make plans to play together afterward, the volunteer notes the arrangement and the newcomer knows who they will join. Take those waiting over to a short activity underway. The host keeps an eye on available places and returns to let them know when a new game can begin.

Seat the new group together for an explanation from the teacher using the printed guide, with questions afterward. Then let everyone suggest moves and watch the teacher try them over a handful of practice turns. They can see together whether the game works as they understood it. Restore the starting position before playing for points. Once play begins, a newcomer can ask the teacher for help at any time. Using what the player can see, explain the tradeoffs and leave the choice in their hands.

Agree when the game must end and write that time in plain view. Designate someone at the table to warn the others in time to save an unfinished game if they wish and put it away. Agree on progress checks as well, using a visible timer. When it sounds, review how far the group has got and the time still available. For a disagreement over a rule, suspend play and give two players a brief chance to consult the rules. The pair report their interpretation to the table. If the text leaves them unsure, their ruling stands for the evening and the question can be researched later.

Arrange a short absence with the other players before heading to the refreshment area. Have the snack or drink there and be back for your next move, which keeps liquids away from the game. For a personal break, tell the others before stepping away and decide together on an immediate stopping point. Be clear about the action due next, conceal private information and hold any play requiring the absent person. During rounds of the room, the host listens for tables whose play is being drowned out. The host can quietly ask the louder group to bring the volume down, sparing the neighboring players that conversation.

As each game ends, have one player work down the contents list aloud. The others gather the pieces, count them and fill the marked bags while the reader ticks off each batch before returning the box. The following day, the host invites guests to answer a brief, optional follow-up about the games on offer, getting a seat and anything that made participation difficult. Guests have time to think back, and grouping their comments when organizing the next event helps the host recognize experiences shared across tables.
