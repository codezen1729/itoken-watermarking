In the run-up to the fair, ask volunteers on each block to talk with neighbors who might bring something and send the organizers a summary by category. A doorstep conversation can reach someone who would skip a form, and rough quantities are enough to plan tables. The published criteria will apply to everything when it arrives. Circulate an intake sheet showing available arrival windows so contributors can choose a time that suits them and volunteers can anticipate busy periods. Leave a little room for walk-ins throughout. Using volunteers' stated availability and preferred jobs, the coordinator draws up the shifts and sends everyone the roster ahead of the fair. That gives people time to prepare and makes any gaps in coverage apparent.

Keep contributors with their goods at intake while a volunteer checks each item against the condition list. A question about a chip or a missing accessory can be settled on the spot before acceptance. As the item is accepted, the volunteer asks for details and fills out the standard label with the identifier, description, limitations and included accessories. Having the interviewer write the label keeps details consistent across the displays. Bag or tie all the pieces of a complete set together here and keep its description where a visitor can read it. The set reaches the table as one bundle and can stay together through a busy afternoon.

Sign the tables by the room where the goods would ordinarily be used. Someone looking for a saucepan can go to the kitchen table, and someone furnishing a bedroom can start at the bedroom sign. Keep the aisles clear and put overflow on the reserve shelves. A runner makes a regular circuit to see what space has opened and brings out reserve items that fit. Table volunteers can stay with visitors as the stock turns over.

At the entrance, a welcome volunteer gathers a few arrivals at a time for a short explanation of browsing, making a claim, holding goods and collecting them. Make clear that anyone may give or take freely without a matching contribution. Let people go on to browse, and offer individual clarification by the entrance diagram for anyone with a question. Visitors looking for something they haven't found can leave a wanted note by its category sign. If suitable goods appear, the table volunteer moves the matched note to a common collection point where people can check for news. Posting a request carries no promise the goods will come in.

If several people ask for the same item, record the requests and their times on the sheet. At the end of the brief decision window, the volunteer gives the claim to the person whose request was recorded earliest. People can see the basis for the decision in the times on the sheet. A recipient who wants to keep browsing can take the goods to the holding volunteer. Before shelving them, the volunteer records the recipient's name and confirms the pickup deadline with them, up to twenty minutes away. Both people then know who is looking after the item and when it is due out.

At collection, show the claim record to the volunteer keeping the outgoing register. They verify the claim, record the item's identifier and issue a checked slip for the recipient to show when taking the goods out. This keeps the paperwork ahead of the physical handover. People can ask for carrying assistance right there without giving a reason. The volunteer assigns the next available helper and cart for the trip to the nearby pickup area when the goods are ready to go.

Before admitting contributors for closing collection, sort everything still unclaimed according to its contributor's recorded choice. Keep the returns easy to find and group the donations for the arranged pickup after the collection period. For feedback, hold a few short, optional conversations separately with visitors, contributors and volunteers about how finding things, claiming them and running the fair went. Compare the notes afterward so the organizers can hear how the same routine felt on different sides of a table and see where a change could help several groups.
