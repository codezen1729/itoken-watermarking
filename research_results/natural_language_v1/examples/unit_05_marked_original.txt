Have the host welcome people in person and ask whether they'd like to play straight away or have a little time to settle in. Writing the name and answer on the welcome sheet helps the host gather the next groups, and a guest arriving alone already knows someone to approach. Invite everyone to look through the collection and put interest markers by games they'd enjoy trying. Use the marks to suggest titles that appeal to the people who have actually arrived. The rest of the available collection is open to requests too.

Ask willing teachers to call out the games they can get going. People can gather around a teacher, see who else is interested and ask what the game involves. The host checks the number against the game's player range before a table begins. Anyone can pass and wait for another start. A welcome volunteer can take someone who comes late over to a table that's nearly finished, introduce them briefly and note any agreement to play in the next game together. Until then, the guest can pick something from the waiting basket. Choose any activity there, put it back as soon as the next table calls, and keep the place already arranged.

At the table, the teacher should talk everyone through the essential rules with the printed aid where people can follow along. Open the floor for questions after everyone has heard the whole explanation. Then give each person a practice turn to try an action that's new to them. Reset the pieces when everyone has had a go so that the first scored choice comes after some hands-on experience. Before play, let each learner who wants strategic help pick a willing partner. When asked, that partner can quietly talk through choices using only information visible to the players concerned. The learner makes the move and knows whom to ask the next time.

Each table writes its agreed stopping time on a card in plain view, leaving time to pack up before closing. Choose someone playing there to give advance notice as the deadline approaches, and agree how to note an unfinished position if people want to save it. For pacing during play, set a timer everyone can see for checkpoints the table chooses together. When it sounds, take a moment to see how much time remains. Nobody has to spend the whole game watching the minutes. If a rule causes a dispute, pause and give two players a short time to look it up. The others can wait for the pair to report back. Accept their reading or play on with their temporary ruling and check the outstanding question after the evening.

Have each table pick someone to listen for rising voices. They can suggest taking a long social conversation to the conversation corner while neighboring players can still hear their own turns. For refreshments, a player can agree a short absence with the group, have food or a drink in the refreshment area and be back for their next action. Anyone needing a personal break should say so aloud. Agree on an immediate place to pause and name the action that will come next. Protect any private information and wait to take actions requiring the absent player.

When a game finishes, have the players sort all the pieces into the labeled bags. The teacher can then verify each bag against the checklist while everyone is still at the table to help find a missing piece. Return the checked box to the collection. The next day, the host sends an optional short prompt about the choice of games, how easy it was to get into one and any difficulty participating. People have a little distance from the evening, and grouping their replies before planning the next one lets the host spot experiences common to several tables.
