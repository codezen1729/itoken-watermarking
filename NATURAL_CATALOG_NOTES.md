# Public catalog for the natural-language follow-up

This catalog replaces artificial proof atoms and global MP/AND/OR route types with ordinary planning decisions. The eight problems concern a library afternoon, reusable-cup lending, a shared garden, museum visits, a craft studio, a board-game evening, a community choir, and a household-goods swap fair. Each problem has sixteen decisions and each decision has three locally plausible alternatives. The alternatives describe a mechanism and its intended consequence in ordinary language.

The two catalog authors composed the problems and alternatives explicitly. Programs serialized their authored text, assigned stable internal identifiers, and checked the schema. They did not make the eight domains by substituting names into one shared template. Several general organizational patterns recur because they occur naturally in these settings: participants choosing before a coordinator assigns, the reverse order, or a joint arrangement. These recurring patterns are a limitation on conceptual diversity, even when their local meaning differs.

The alternatives usually share their principal concepts. Their differences concern who initiates a choice, the order of information and action, how resources or work are allocated, what evidence is considered first, or how an intermediate decision leads to an outcome. There is no global relation between an option's position and a particular inference rule or watermark bit. The catalog's IDs are computation and annotation handles only. They are not to appear in generated or reworded prose. The `application` field concatenates the explicitly authored `mechanism` and `consequence`; its context is supplied separately by the current decision.

The problem statements allow several workable approaches and avoid requiring one specific option at another decision. A complete selected set can therefore be developed into a proposal without a fixed earlier route. This is a design intention checked by the authors, not an exhaustive proof that every combination is equally good. The candidate consequences are plausible planning rationales, not guaranteed empirical outcomes. The experiment is not a field trial of the proposed services.

These are curated semantic alternatives, not newly proved deductions in a symbolic calculus. An iToken in this follow-up identifies a selected local mechanism and its consequence under a known question and context. Its legitimacy as the writer's asserted choice is judged from the source. A reader merely mentioning an action, rejecting it, considering it hypothetically, or reporting that somebody else proposed it has not necessarily adopted that choice. Recovery should preserve such distinctions and should not fill missing choices from the public catalog.

Removing artificial symbols removes the previous deterministic atom-to-route shortcut. It does not guarantee that ordinary words carry no shortcuts. Some mechanisms naturally need distinctive concepts, and writers might copy a catalog phrase unless instructed to use their own wording. Free rewording must therefore be permitted to change names for ordinary concepts, sentence order, and phrasing; it must not be required to preserve a route-specific keyword. Lexical baselines and cases that mention an option without adopting it remain useful checks. Catalog design alone cannot certify that semantic interpretation is necessary for successful recovery.

Each scored option has one textual realization in this catalog. Normalizing its model sequence probability among the three listed applications produces a probability for predicting the next catalog choice under that local menu. It does not marginalize over every paraphrase, eliminate differences in sequence length, or recover an unrestricted distribution over ideas. Conditioning on a known problem and candidate vocabulary also makes recovery easier than discovering a new ontology or resolving an arbitrary real-world argument.

The catalog must be frozen before any probability or recovery outcomes are examined. Any later content revision needs a different catalog hash and a new experiment. The ordinary-language surface is a substantive change from explicit proof symbols, but the resulting evidence remains a bounded-catalog test rather than general idea watermarking.

Final structural checks found eight problems, 128 distinct decisions and contexts, 384 distinct applications, and 520 globally unique internal identifiers. Applications contain 16–39 whitespace-delimited words (mean 29.52). The catalog passes the planner schema and request-construction checks, and none of its application texts contains an internal identifier or the previous artificial proof atoms. A review of the local contexts found no obvious mutually exclusive cross-decision commitments; this remains an author review rather than exhaustive combination validation.
